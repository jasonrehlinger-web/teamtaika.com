Language Access for Nonprofits: What Accepting Federal Funding Actually Obligates You To Do

Jason R. Ehlinger·October 20, 2026·8 min read

The executive director of a small food-pantry network signed a pass-through grant agreement last spring. The money came from a state agency, which received it from a federal program. Nobody on staff thought of the organization as a "federal contractor," and nobody read the civil-rights assurances on page nine. Then a neighbor who speaks Haitian Creole was turned away from an intake desk because no one could explain the paperwork, and a complaint reached the state agency. The director's first reaction was the one most nonprofits share: "But we're not the government."

That sentence is the misconception. Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 does not attach to government agencies as such. It attaches to recipients of federal financial assistance — and that includes private and nonprofit organizations, and the subrecipients one step further down the funding chain.

Quick answer: If your nonprofit receives federal financial assistance, directly or as a subrecipient of a state or local pass-through grant, Title VI of the Civil Rights Act (42 U.S.C. § 2000d) prohibits national-origin discrimination in your programs, and federal agencies have long read that to require reasonable steps to give limited-English-proficient (LEP) people meaningful access. In practice that means assessing which languages your community speaks, providing qualified interpreters and translated vital documents where the need is real, telling people the help is free, and documenting what you did. Health-related programs face additional rules under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act.

Why "we're just a small nonprofit" doesn't remove the obligation

Three features of the law catch nonprofits off guard.

  • Coverage follows the money, not the org chart. HHS's Title VI guidance for recipients of federal financial assistance states that subrecipients are covered when federal funds are passed through from one recipient to another. A county contract funded by a federal block grant can bring your organization in without a federal check ever bearing your name.
  • It reaches the whole program, not just the funded line item. Under the Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987, coverage extends to the recipient's entire "program or activity." HHS's guidance notes this holds even when only one part of the organization receives the assistance.
  • The legal footing is old. The Supreme Court held in Lau v. Nichols (1974) that a school district's failure to help non-English-speaking students violated Title VI. Agency regulations, such as HHS's at 45 C.F.R. § 80.3(b)(2), then turned that principle into obligations for funded organizations.

A necessary caveat about what changed in 2025

You may have read that "language access requirements were repealed." That is half true, and the half that is false is the expensive half. Executive Order 13166, which directed federal agencies to issue LEP guidance, was revoked in March 2025 by Executive Order 14224. But an executive order was never the source of the obligation. Title VI is a statute, Lau is still the law, and funding agencies' regulations and grant terms did not vanish with the order. What has become less predictable is how aggressively particular agencies enforce, and which guidance documents they still publish.

For a nonprofit, the practical reading is conservative: your award agreement and its civil-rights assurances are the document that binds you. Read them, and ask your funder what language-access expectations apply. We would rather you hear that from a primary source than from a vendor, including us.

An organization that built a defensible language access practice in 2023 and quietly dropped it in 2025 has not reduced its risk. It has removed the evidence that it was acting reasonably.

What "reasonable steps" actually means

Federal guidance does not demand that every nonprofit translate everything into every language. HHS's Title VI guidance describes a flexible, four-factor assessment and explicitly aims not to impose undue burdens on small nonprofits. The factors are:

  1. The number or proportion of LEP people eligible to be served or likely to encounter your program.
  2. How frequently they come into contact with it.
  3. The nature and importance of the program, activity, or service to people's lives.
  4. The resources available to you and the cost of providing assistance.

The third factor is where nonprofits underestimate themselves. A volunteer-run book club and an emergency rental-assistance program are both nonprofits, but a missed deadline in the second can mean an eviction. The higher the stakes of the service, the more the guidance expects.

The villains: four shortcuts that fail audits

1. The bilingual volunteer

Fluency is not interpreting. HHS's guidance warns that bilingual staff are not automatically qualified interpreters and cautions against relying on family members, especially children. A volunteer who speaks Spanish at home can mistranslate an eligibility rule, and your organization owns the error. (We cover this in depth in Bilingual Staff Are Not Interpreters.)

2. Free machine translation on vital documents

Pasting a consent form into a consumer tool is cheap and fast. It also produces text nobody has reviewed, in a document the client may sign. Machine output can be a useful starting point inside a human-reviewed workflow. It is not a finished translation of something that affects someone's benefits or rights.

3. The one-time translation that goes stale

A form translated in 2022 and never updated when the eligibility rules changed is worse than no form, because it is wrong with confidence. Translated materials need an owner and a revision trigger.

4. No record of any of it

If a funder or a civil-rights office asks what you did, "we try our best" is not an answer. A short written assessment, a list of translated documents, and an interpreter-request log are.

If you run a health-related program

Nonprofits that operate clinics, behavioral-health services, or other health programs receiving federal financial assistance face a second layer. Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act and its implementing regulations at 45 C.F.R. Part 92 require meaningful access for LEP individuals and set a standard for qualified interpreters and translators. If this is you, see our guide to Section 1557 qualified interpreter requirements before you design your plan.

Failure versus success, concretely

Failure looks like the pantry director's story: an unreviewed volunteer, an unread assurance clause, a complaint with nothing in the file to answer it. Even if the complaint is ultimately resolved, the funder now has a reason to look closely at everything else, and an agreement that can be conditioned or not renewed is a real threat to a small organization.

Success looks quieter. A client asks for help in Haitian Creole, the front desk uses the interpreter line, the intake form is already translated, and the log gets one more line. Nobody writes about it, which is the point. The same practice also widens your reach: the people you could not serve before become people you can, and many funders now ask about reach in the application itself.
