The distinction that protects your budget

Title I is the flexible, defensible vehicle for supplemental language access.

The core civil-rights language access a district must provide for English learners — what Title VI and the Equal Educational Opportunities Act already require — generally cannot be charged to Title III, and is not reliably fundable through Title I either, because federal cost principles forbid using federal funds to pay for something a district must provide anyway. That is the supplement-not-supplant rule.

Title I, Part A is the more flexible and defensible vehicle for supplemental Parent and Family Engagement activities, Title I-specific communications, and supports tied to a need identified in the schoolwide plan. This page shows you exactly where the law allows it — and how to document every dollar so it survives a state monitoring visit or audit.

Why this matters for Title I achievement goals

Access barriers are an achievement barrier — not a side issue.

When parents cannot read the compact they are asked to sign, or cannot follow the meeting where their child's reading plan is explained, the achievement gap Title I exists to close stays open. ESSA treats language and access barriers as part of Title I's statutory purpose of closing achievement gaps, not as an administrative afterthought.

ESSA explicitly includes English learners and students with disabilities in school accountability and in the Parent and Family Engagement requirements of Section 1116. Addressing their access to Title I programs sits squarely inside the law's intent — a district that removes those barriers is advancing the exact outcomes its Title I plan is measured against.

The question a Title I coordinator actually needs answered is simple: can we use Title I for this? The answer is a confident, cited yes — within clear limits. Here is exactly where the law allows it, and how to document it.

Federal compliance

Where the law allows it — with the citation, inline.

Four points carry the entire funding argument. Each one is stated with its statutory or regulatory citation so your business office and your state monitor are looking at the same source you are.

1. Parent and Family Engagement — ESSA Section 1116 (20 U.S.C. § 6318)

Under Section 1116, LEAs and schools must provide Title I information to parents in a format and, to the extent practicable, in a language parents can understand — and this requirement explicitly extends to parents with limited English proficiency, parents with disabilities, and parents of migratory children (Section 1116(f)). The 1 percent Parent and Family Engagement reservation applies to any LEA receiving more than $500,000 in Title I, Part A funds (Section 1116(a)(3)): at least 90 percent of the reserved amount must go to schools, and the LEA may retain up to 10 percent for district-level parent and family engagement activities. This set-aside is one of the cleanest places to fund translation and interpretation of Title I policies, meetings, school-parent compacts, and capacity-building activities — because the activities it funds are, by definition, Title I parent engagement activities. An LEA at or below the $500,000 threshold has no mandatory set-aside but still must meet the underlying Section 1116 requirements, so the same translation and interpretation needs still exist, just without a dedicated minimum budget line.

2. Schoolwide vs. targeted assistance programs

Schoolwide programs have broader flexibility: funds may support any activity identified in the comprehensive needs assessment and the schoolwide plan, provided it is necessary, reasonable, and allocable to the program. Targeted assistance programs are more restrictive and require that funded services primarily benefit students formally identified for Title I services. In both program types, Title I funds must supplement, not supplant the non-Federal funds a district would otherwise be required to spend — and that non-Federal baseline must still fully cover the legally required services for English learners and students with disabilities. In targeted assistance programs, the supplement test applies at the individual student level; in schoolwide programs, it applies at the program level. This is the single most common point a state monitor probes, and overstating schoolwide flexibility is the fastest way to lose a Title I coordinator's trust — so we document the trace-back on every engagement.

3. Explicitly allowable examples, drawn from state guidance

Confirmed examples of Title I-allowable translation and interpretation activities, drawn from published guidance in Georgia, Kansas, and Maine, include:

  • Interpretation and translation of Title I-specific communications — the Title I newsletter, required Title I notifications, and the Annual Title I Meeting — limited to Title I schools and Title I-sponsored or Title I-required activities.
  • Interpretation at meetings that explain Title I requirements to parents, including school-parent compacts and parent and family engagement policies.
  • Translation and interpretation that enables parents to participate in Title I parent and family engagement activities and schoolwide plan input sessions.
Important limit

If a district offers Title I-style activities or materials at a non-Title I school, translation and interpretation for that activity becomes the district's own responsibility under Title VI and must be paid with local funds — not Title I. We flag this for you up front; it protects the district's audit position as much as our credibility.

4. Title I vs. Title III — at a glance

This page is intended as a practical guide to allowable uses of Title I, Part A funds for language access and accessibility services. It is not legal advice.
