Translation Services for Legal Matters: What Courts Actually Accept

Jason R. Ehlinger·November 12, 2026·8 min read

Most people shopping for translation services for legal matters are carrying two assumptions, and both are wrong. The first is that any fluent person's translation is good enough to file, as long as somebody signs it. The second is that if you win, the other side pays for it. In federal court, the Supreme Court took the usual route for recovering it off the table in 2012. The first one is settled every week, one challenged exhibit at a time.

Picture the paralegal with a Spanish-language contract due as an exhibit on Friday, or the immigration attorney with forty pages of Vietnamese civil records, or the general counsel whose foreign subsidiary just got served. A bilingual associate offers to "just translate it." It feels free. It is the most expensive way to do it.

Quick answer: A translated document used in a legal matter is judged by where it is going. USCIS requires a complete English translation with the translator's signed certification that it is accurate and that they are competent to translate (8 CFR 103.2(b)(3)). Courts generally expect the original alongside a translation whose accuracy a qualified translator will attest to, and the court — not the vendor — decides whether it comes in. In federal court, document translation is not a taxable cost under 28 U.S.C. § 1920(6) (Taniguchi v. Kan Pacific Saipan, 2012), so absent a fee-shifting statute or contract clause, plan on bearing it — and get it right the first time.

If you already know what you need and want it done, Taika's translation services for legal documents — contracts, court filings, depositions and discovery, certified for submission — are the place to start a quote. If you want to understand what "certified" actually has to mean for your matter first, read on.

"Certified" Means Something Specific — and It Isn't a License

There is no federal license for document translators in the United States, and no government body that "certifies" a translation before you file it. In a legal setting, a certified translation is a translation accompanied by a signed statement from the translator (or the translation company) attesting that it is complete and accurate and that the translator is competent in both languages. That statement is what makes the translation something a person is accountable for.

That accountability is the point. If opposing counsel challenges a translated exhibit, the question becomes who made the translation, what their qualifications are, and whether they will stand behind it — potentially under penalty of perjury in a declaration, or on the witness stand. A translator who works for your firm, or a friend of the client, is a weaker witness on all three counts than an independent professional with a documented process.

What Each Destination Actually Requires

The same document can need different things depending on where it is headed. Here is the practical map:

Where the document goes What it typically needs
USCIS filing A full English translation of the entire document, with the translator's certification that it is complete and accurate and that they are competent to translate from the foreign language into English — 8 CFR 103.2(b)(3). Summaries and extracts do not qualify.
Court exhibit (federal or state) The original document with an accurate translation, usually supported by a translator's certificate, declaration or affidavit. Check the court's local rules. Admissibility is the court's call, and the other side can contest the translation.
Service of process abroad Under Article 5 of the Hague Service Convention, the receiving country's Central Authority may require the documents to be in, or translated into, its official language. Requirements vary by country — check that country's declaration before you send.
Contracts and commercial agreements Precision in terms of art, defined terms, dates and figures. If the agreement exists in two languages, its language clause decides which version controls — the translation has to be consistent with that choice.
Depositions, hearings and trials That is interpretation (spoken), not translation (written). Federal Rule of Evidence 604 requires an interpreter to be qualified and to take an oath to make a true translation.

Notice what is missing from that table: a notary stamp. Notarization verifies a signature, not a translation, and only some recipients ask for it. When a court or agency does require it, it is an add-on to the translator's certification, never a substitute for it.

The Cost Nobody Budgets For: It Usually Isn't Taxable

Federal law lets the prevailing party recover certain costs, including "compensation of interpreters" under 28 U.S.C. § 1920(6). For years, some courts read that to include document translation. In Taniguchi v. Kan Pacific Saipan, Ltd., 566 U.S. 560 (2012), the Supreme Court held otherwise: "interpreters" means oral translation, so the cost of translating written documents is not a taxable cost under that statute.

The practical effect is blunt. In federal court, unless a fee-shifting statute, a contract clause or a sanctions order covers it, plan on the document translation bill being yours whether you win or lose. That changes the calculus in two ways. It makes the cheapest option tempting — and it makes a rejected or impeached translation doubly expensive, because you pay for the bad one, pay again for the replacement, and pay for the motion practice in between.

The question isn't "what's the least we can spend on this translation?" It's "what's the least we can spend on this translation once?"

The Shortcuts That Come Back to Bite

The bilingual associate or staff member

Fluency is not translation skill, and conversational Spanish is not legal Spanish. Legal translation means carrying a foreign legal concept into English without silently converting it into a U.S. concept that means something different. It also means the translator is now a potential witness — an awkward position for someone on your payroll.

Machine translation passed off as finished work

Free machine translation shifts verb tenses in testimony, flattens defined terms, and mangles names and dates — exactly the details a cross-examination targets. There is also a confidentiality question worth asking before anyone pastes discovery or privileged material into a free consumer tool: where does that text go, and who agreed to its terms of use?

The lowest bid on a mixed document set

A matter rarely involves one document. Split forty exhibits across three freelancers and you get three spellings of the same party's name and two translations of the same contract term. Consistency across a document set is something opposing counsel notices — and exploits.

Translation or Interpretation? Get the Right One Booked

Written documents need translation. Depositions, client meetings, hearings and arbitrations need interpretation — consecutive or simultaneous, in person or by video. Many matters need both, and they often need them on the same deadline. Our legal and court language services page covers both sides, and our interpretation services cover phone, video and on-site work for attorney-client meetings and proceedings.

A Three-Step Plan That Holds Up

  1. Name the destination first. USCIS, a specific court, a foreign Central Authority, or a counterparty — each sets the requirements. Look up the local rule or country declaration before you order, not after the filing is bounced.
  2. Send the whole set, with the deadline. Bundle every exhibit and record for the matter so one team handles terminology, names and dates consistently. Include any glossary or prior translations the case already relies on.
  3. Get certified delivery that matches the destination. A signed certification of accuracy and competence on every document, notarization where the recipient requires it, and source and translation kept paired so the record is easy to follow.

Skip these and the failure mode is familiar: a challenged exhibit, a request for evidence, a returned service request, a hearing continued while someone scrambles for a replacement — all paid for out of pocket. Do them and the translation becomes the least interesting thing about your filing, which is exactly what it should be.
